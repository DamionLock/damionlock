My AI Infrastructure Thesis: Start With the Workload, Not the Building

A point of view I'll be held to for twelve months

Damion Lock · Medium · scheduled Wed, 4 Nov 2026 09:00 · DL-MED-001

Every data centre I have ever designed began on the same page: a site plan. A plot, a slab, a power feed, and a date two or three years out when the first rack would finally hum. After 36 years in infrastructure and something like 45 of those designs, I have come to a conclusion I would have argued against for most of my career. The site plan is the wrong first page.

This is my AI infrastructure thesis, set out plainly so you can hold me to it over the next twelve months.

The building was always the slow part

A conventional data centre takes eighteen to thirty-six months from the decision to live capacity. For most of my working life that was fine. Demand grew in curves you could roughly forecast, so you built ahead and were approximately right.

AI has broken that arithmetic. The workloads an organisation needs now show up faster than any construction programme can serve, and they show up in places a central campus cannot reach: a hospital, a port, a factory, a government building that already exists. The waiting used to be invisible because everyone waited. Now the waiting is the cost.

Move the compute, not the data

The old model shipped your data to the compute. Collect everything, send it to a distant region, process it there, pay the bill. The mobile data centre model turns that around. You bring pre-engineered compute to where the data and the people already are.

I want to be precise about what I mean, because "modular data centre" gets used loosely. I mean a complete facility, power, cooling, fire, security and monitoring included, engineered and tested before it leaves the factory, delivered as units you can place in a car park, a plant room or an existing hall. It is not a cupboard with a rack in it. It is a data centre that happens not to be married to a slab.

Three things this changes

Speed. When the hard engineering is done once, upstream, what happens on site is connection rather than construction. Weeks, not years.

Right-sizing. You add capacity in increments instead of betting the budget on one forecast. If the forecast is wrong, and it usually is, you have not poured it into concrete.

Location. Compute can sit next to data that is too sensitive, too large or too latency-bound to send away. For a lot of organisations in the Gulf, India and Europe, that is no longer a preference. It is a regulatory fact.

Where I expect to be wrong

The hyperscale campus is not going anywhere. For certain workloads it is exactly the right answer, and I would be a poor engineer to pretend otherwise. My claim is narrower: the giant building should stop being the default first answer. For a growing share of real needs, mobile and modular is simply the better fit, and I would rather make that case in public and be corrected than keep it in a proposal nobody reads.

I also expect to be wrong on timing. Edge computing has been "two years away" for a decade. What is different now is that the workload finally justifies it.

What this series will cover

Over the coming year I will take this apart piece by piece. Where your most valuable data should physically live. When owned AI infrastructure beats a cloud bill. How a deployment collapses from months to weeks. The water question almost nobody asks. What my 45 designs actually taught me, including the expensive lessons. And whether the building you already own is closer to AI-ready than you think.

I am an engineer, not a natural writer, so I am treating this as building in public. The thesis is on the table. Pull it apart.

If you were asked to stand up serious AI capacity tomorrow, what would your first page be: the site plan, or the workload?
